Overview

Sahil and I designed this arm almost completely from scratch: every arm part, linkage and assembly, the cycloidal actuators and the equations that define their curves, the motor driver circuitry and PCB, the forward kinematics tele-op controller, and much of the code.

What we actually made is a framework for building 6DOF arms, which we called Verso. There are two arms, mine (Arm N) and Sahil's (Arm S). They look different, but they're built from the same parts and ideas and borrow pieces from each other. I modeled about half of the parts and Sahil modeled the other half. We open-sourced all of it at versorobotics.com:

The arm

My arm uses three NEMA 17 stepper motors, two NEMA 11s and a NEMA 8. DOF 2, 3 and 5 are driven through belt-and-pulley stages.

Almost everything is 3D printed. The joints carry the cycloidal actuators below, with the bearings, limit switches and fasteners hidden inside the printed housings. The base under DOF 1 is a good example: with the housing made see-through, you can see the actuator, an axial bearing, the radial bearings and a limit switch packed inside it.

Cycloidal actuators

We needed reducers to increase our NEMA stepper motors' torque output and precision. Rather than buy them, I generated them myself, partly to learn function driven modeling. I couldn't find cycloidal gear profiles in our size range anyway.

I made a parametric cycloidal gear generator with the Desmos API, set up so its equations can go straight into Fusion 360's Equation Driven Curve add-in. All of the actuators in the arm came from it (more on the math on the generator page).

The result is a 28:1 cycloidal gearbox with nearly zero backlash that's still backdrivable.

The actuator parts are laser cut, which is why everything in these photos is see-through. They can be 3D printed instead if laser cutting isn't an option. I made a video about the generator and the actuators:

Electronics

Everything runs off a custom PCB built around a Teensy 4.1. It has six A4988 stepper drivers, with jumpers to set the microstepping, inputs for up to nine limit switches with debouncing on each, and connectors for a rotary encoder, an OLED screen over I2C and a Bluetooth module. The 12 V supply runs the motors and is stepped down to 5 V for the Teensy.

The PCB design was mostly Sahil's work. I helped with the component selection and high-level design, and wired and soldered everything on my arm. The board sits in the base.

Tele-op controller

The controller is a miniature version of the arm, made by scaling down the CAD of the big one and swapping the actuators for 10k potentiometers. You move its joints by hand and the arm copies them, which only needs forward kinematics since the joints match one to one.

Inverse kinematics (giving the arm a target and solving for the joint angles) is one of the things we didn't build from scratch, but it's something I want to come back to.

Build

The full bill of materials on the Verso site comes to about $330, electronics included. The arm is mostly 3D printed, with laser cut parts for the cycloidal actuators.

It's by far the most time I've spent on any project. We each spent hundreds of hours on it across a full year of planning, designing, testing, failing and replanning.
