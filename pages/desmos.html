Four numbers in, a gear out

A cycloidal gear generator I built on the Desmos API. You give it four numbers and it gives back the disc profile as a pair of parametric equations, x(t) and y(t), written in exactly the form Fusion 360's Equation Driven Curve add-in takes. I made it to design the actuators for my 6DOF arm, and every one of them came out of it.

The rest of this page is the geometry behind those equations: where the curve comes from, why the pin radius shows up in it, where it stops working, and how a wobbling disc becomes a 27:1 gearbox.

A circle rolling around a circle

Ignore the pins for a second. Roll a small circle of radius R/N around the outside of a fixed circle of radius R(N − 1)/N, and follow a point stuck to the small circle at distance E from its centre. That point traces an epitrochoid.

The small circle's circumference fits around the fixed one exactly N − 1 times, so the curve closes after N − 1 lobes. Keeping E smaller than the rolling radius keeps the point inside the small circle, and the lobes stay smooth.

x₀(t) = R cos t − E cos(Nt)
y₀(t) = −R sin t + E sin(Nt)
The first term carries the rolling circle's centre around at radius R. The second spins the point around that centre N times as fast.

Making room for round pins

That curve is where the centres of the pins need to be. The pins aren't points, though. They're cylinders of radius S, so the disc has to follow the edge that every pin position touches: the curve moved inward by S, perpendicular to itself at every point.

That's the extra term in the generator. b is the angle of the curve's normal at t, so the S terms step each point inward by exactly the pin radius:

x(t) = R cos t − S cos(t − b) − E cos(Nt)
y(t) = −R sin t + S sin(t − b) + E sin(Nt)
b(t) = −arctan sin((1 − N)t)R/(EN) − cos((1 − N)t)
Checked numerically against the generator's output: the step is S long to within rounding, and always perpendicular to the base curve.

Where it breaks

Not every set of numbers gives you a disc you can cut. Two limits matter.

Eccentricity: EN/R < 1

EN/R is the eccentricity divided by the rolling circle's radius. As it climbs toward 1, the valleys between lobes get sharper. At exactly 1 they become cusps, and past 1 the curve loops over itself.

The arctan in b knows this too. Its denominator, R/(EN) − cos(…), can only reach zero or below once EN/R ≥ 1. The same condition that ruins the shape also breaks the equation.

Pin size: S < ρ at the lobe tips

Moving the curve inward by S only works if S is smaller than the tightest radius of curvature on the lobe tips. Go past that and the offset folds over itself, which is undercut.

Deeper lobes have tighter tips. With N = 10, that limit is 0.32 R at EN/R = 0.65, but only 0.13 R at 0.95.

The generator's defaults sit well inside both: E = S/2 works out to EN/R ≈ 0.78, and the pin radius is about half of what would start to undercut.

Why it's a gearbox

Put the disc inside a ring of N pins and push its centre around a small circle of radius E. That's the job of the eccentric on the input shaft. The disc can't spin freely because its lobes are sitting between the pins. It can only roll.

It has N − 1 lobes in N gaps, so each time the input goes around once, the disc comes back one lobe behind where it started. With the pins fixed, the disc turns once, the other way, for every N − 1 turns of the input.

The minus sign is the direction flip. With the default 28 pins that's 27:1 from a single stage. The simulation above runs on the generator's own equations, and in this ideal geometry every pin is touching the disc at every moment.

It can animate the disc, and there's a slider for the pin count. There are also a lot of hidden functions in it. Those are leftovers from versions that worked but didn't drop into Fusion cleanly. I kept them, switched off, in case you want to see simpler ways to build an epitrochoid.
